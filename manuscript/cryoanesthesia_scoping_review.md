# Cryoanalgesia as an alternative to local anaesthetic drugs during supply chain disruptions: a scoping review

## Structured Abstract

**Background:** Global shortages of local anaesthetic drugs, driven by manufacturing disruptions, regulatory non-compliance at Indian pharmaceutical facilities, and supply chain vulnerabilities, have created an urgent need for alternative analgesic modalities. Cryoanalgesia --- the application of cold to achieve pain relief --- represents a potential drug-free alternative. We conducted a scoping review to map the existing evidence comparing cryoanalgesia with local anaesthetic drugs across clinical domains.

**Methods:** This scoping review followed the JBI methodology and PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) reporting guidelines. We searched MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception to March 2026. We included all study designs comparing any cryoanalgesic modality (ice, vapocoolant spray, cryoprobe, percutaneous cryoneurolysis) with any local anaesthetic drug or formulation for pain management. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review. Data were extracted using a standardised charting form and synthesised narratively.

**Results:** From 1247 records identified, 47 studies met inclusion criteria (28 randomised controlled trials, 8 systematic reviews/meta-analyses, 6 retrospective cohort studies, 5 case series/reports). Evidence was mapped across five clinical domains: (i) dental/oral procedures (n=14), where ice and ethyl chloride spray showed non-inferiority to topical benzocaine or lidocaine gel for pre-injection analgesia; (ii) vascular access procedures (n=12), where vapocoolant sprays were effective in adults but not in children; (iii) thoracic surgery (n=11), where intercostal cryoneurolysis reduced opioid consumption compared with standard care but direct comparisons with local anaesthetic-based nerve blocks were lacking; (iv) orthopaedic surgery (n=6), where cryoneurolysis showed promise for prolonged analgesia following total knee arthroplasty; and (v) dermatological procedures (n=4), where ice packs demonstrated comparable efficacy to topical lidocaine-prilocaine cream. No study explicitly examined cryoanalgesia as an alternative during local anaesthetic shortages.

**Conclusions:** Cryoanalgesia is a viable drug-free alternative to local anaesthetics for superficial procedures, with the strongest evidence in dental pre-injection analgesia and vascular access in adults. Critical evidence gaps remain for deeper surgical applications where cryoneurolysis and local anaesthetic nerve blocks have not been directly compared. Given ongoing pharmaceutical supply chain vulnerabilities, prospective studies evaluating cryoanalgesia as a contingency strategy during local anaesthetic shortages are warranted.

**Keywords:** cryoanalgesia; cryoanesthesia; cryoneurolysis; local anaesthetics; drug shortage; supply chain; scoping review

## Introduction

Local anaesthetic drugs are among the most frequently used medications in healthcare, underpinning an enormous range of procedures from minor outpatient interventions to major surgery.^1^ The global market encompasses lidocaine, bupivacaine, ropivacaine, and articaine, among others, with demand spanning emergency medicine, dentistry, dermatology, obstetrics, and perioperative care.

In recent years, the reliability of this supply has been seriously compromised. Since mid-2023, the United States has experienced persistent shortages of lidocaine with epinephrine, primarily attributed to manufacturing delays at major suppliers including Pfizer.^2^ The American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP) has continuously listed bupivacaine injection as a drug in shortage.^3^ As of June 2025, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) listed 194 drugs in active shortage, including several injectable anaesthetics.^4^

The European Union (EU) faces a distinct but related vulnerability rooted in its dependence on generic pharmaceutical manufacturing in India. In 2015, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) recommended suspending marketing authorisations for over 700 generic medicines following Good Clinical Practice (GCP) violations at GVK Biosciences in Hyderabad, India.^5^ This pattern recurred in 2023 when the EMA recommended suspension of approximately 400 generic medicines tested by Synapse Labs Pvt. Ltd in Pune, India, after Spanish regulators identified critical irregularities in bioequivalence study data.^6,7^ These regulatory actions directly reduced the availability of multiple pharmaceutical products in European markets, and the precedent they set continues to create supply uncertainty. Additional Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) violations at Indian facilities, including cross-contamination risks and data integrity failures, have compounded this instability.^8^

The convergence of manufacturing concentration, regulatory vulnerability, and logistic fragility means that local anaesthetic shortages are not isolated incidents but a structural feature of the current pharmaceutical landscape.^9^ The International Pain and Spine Intervention Society (IPSIS) issued a shortage advisory in 2025, highlighting the clinical impact on interventional pain procedures.^10^ The BMJ published an editorial in January 2025 calling drug shortages "an urgent priority" and noting that supply disruption notifications in the UK doubled between 2022 and 2024.^11^

Cryoanalgesia --- the therapeutic application of cold to produce analgesia --- offers a mechanistically distinct, drug-free alternative. The spectrum of cryoanalgesic modalities ranges from simple surface cooling (ice application, vapocoolant sprays) to sophisticated percutaneous cryoneurolysis devices that selectively ablate peripheral nerves under ultrasound guidance.^12^ The underlying principle is cold-induced nerve conduction blockade: at temperatures below 10 degrees C, peripheral nerve transmission is reversibly inhibited, while at -20 degrees C to -100 degrees C, Wallerian degeneration occurs with preservation of the endoneurial sheath, allowing eventual nerve regeneration.^13^ Depending on the modality, analgesic duration ranges from minutes (surface cooling) to weeks or months (cryoneurolysis).^14^

Despite the growing evidence base for cryoanalgesia in several clinical domains, no study has systematically mapped the breadth and depth of evidence comparing cryoanalgesic modalities with local anaesthetic drugs, nor has any study examined cryoanalgesia explicitly as a supply chain contingency. We therefore conducted a scoping review to: (i) identify and characterise studies comparing cryoanalgesia with local anaesthetic drugs; (ii) map the evidence across clinical domains, populations, and outcomes; and (iii) identify gaps in the existing literature to guide future research prioritisation, particularly in the context of pharmaceutical supply chain resilience.

## Methods

### Protocol and registration

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews^15^ and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist.^16^ The protocol was developed a priori.

### Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria:

**Population:** Patients of any age undergoing any procedure or experiencing any pain condition requiring local or regional analgesia.

**Concept:** Any form of cryoanalgesia (ice application, vapocoolant spray [ethyl chloride, tetrafluoroethane, alkane mixtures], cooled local anaesthetic solution, cryoprobe neurolysis, percutaneous cryoneurolysis) compared with any local anaesthetic drug or formulation (injectable, topical gel, cream, or patch) for pain management.

**Context:** Any clinical setting (hospital, outpatient, emergency department, dental clinic, operating theatre). No restrictions on geographic location.

**Study design:** All study designs were eligible, including randomised controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental studies, cohort studies, case-control studies, case series, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. Narrative reviews were included only for contextual mapping. Case reports with fewer than three patients, conference abstracts without full text, letters, editorials, and animal studies were excluded.

**Language:** English-language publications. Non-English publications with English abstracts were screened and included if sufficient data were available in the abstract.

**Time frame:** No date restrictions.

### Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across five electronic databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase (via Ovid), the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science Core Collection, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The search was conducted in March 2026.

The search strategy combined three concept blocks using Boolean operators:

**Block 1 (Cryoanalgesia):** cryoanalgesia OR cryoanesthesia OR cryoanaesthesia OR cryoneurolysis OR cryoablation OR cryotherapy OR "vapocoolant spray" OR "ethyl chloride spray" OR "cold spray" OR "ice application" OR "pre-cooling" OR "precooling" OR "topical cooling" OR "refrigerant spray"

**Block 2 (Local anaesthetics):** "local anesthetic" OR "local anaesthetic" OR lidocaine OR lignocaine OR bupivacaine OR ropivacaine OR articaine OR prilocaine OR mepivacaine OR benzocaine OR "EMLA" OR "topical anesthetic" OR "topical anaesthetic" OR "nerve block"

**Block 3 (Comparison/Pain):** pain OR analgesia OR anaesthesia OR anesthesia OR "pain management" OR "pain relief" OR "pain control"

The final search combined Block 1 AND Block 2, with Block 3 used to refine results where initial yields were excessive. Reference lists of included systematic reviews were hand-searched for additional relevant studies.

### Study selection

Records were imported into a reference management database and deduplicated. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant records were retrieved and assessed independently by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

### Data charting

Data were extracted using a standardised charting form adapted from the JBI template for scoping reviews.^15^ The following variables were extracted: author(s), year, country, study design, sample size, clinical domain, population characteristics (age, sex, clinical indication), cryoanalgesic modality (type, temperature, duration of application), comparator (local anaesthetic drug, formulation, dose, route), primary outcome(s), secondary outcome(s), key findings, and study limitations.

### Data synthesis

Findings were synthesised narratively and organised by clinical domain. Quantitative pooling was not performed, consistent with scoping review methodology. Results were presented in tabular and diagrammatic formats to facilitate mapping of the evidence landscape.

## Results

### Search results

The database search yielded 1247 records after deduplication. After title and abstract screening, 134 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 47 studies met the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). The most common reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were: no direct comparison between cryoanalgesia and a local anaesthetic drug (n=41), duplicate data from the same study population (n=12), conference abstracts without full text (n=9), and non-human studies (n=6).

### Study characteristics

The 47 included studies comprised 28 RCTs, 8 systematic reviews or meta-analyses, 6 retrospective cohort studies, and 5 case series. Studies were published between 1976 and 2025, with a marked increase in publications after 2020 (n=24, 51%). Geographic distribution spanned 19 countries, with the largest contributions from the United States (n=10), India (n=9), and the United Kingdom (n=4).

Studies were mapped across five clinical domains (Table 1): dental and oral procedures (n=14, 30%); vascular access procedures (n=12, 26%); thoracic surgery and chest wall trauma (n=11, 23%); orthopaedic surgery (n=6, 13%); and dermatological procedures (n=4, 9%).

### Domain 1: Dental and oral procedures (n=14)

This domain contained the most direct head-to-head comparisons between cryoanalgesia and local anaesthetic drugs, with 11 RCTs and 3 systematic reviews (Table 2).

**Ice application versus topical anaesthetic gel for pre-injection analgesia.** Seven RCTs compared ice or ice stick application with topical anaesthetic gel (benzocaine 20% or lidocaine 5%) applied to the oral mucosa before local anaesthetic injection. Rai and colleagues^17^ conducted a split-mouth RCT in 60 patients receiving palatal injections and found no significant difference between ice and 20% benzocaine gel (visual analogue scale [VAS]: 11 vs 10; *P*=0.955). Chilakamuri and colleagues^18^ similarly reported comparable pain scores between pre-cooling with ice and 5% lignocaine gel in a split-mouth crossover trial of 30 children aged 7--9 years. Hindocha and colleagues^19^ compared ice with lidocaine 5% gel for oral mucosal anaesthesia in a crossover RCT and reported equivalent pin-prick pain scores.

**Ethyl chloride spray versus topical anaesthetic gel.** Abbasi and colleagues^20^ randomised 90 patients to ethyl chloride spray or 5% lidocaine gel before buccal infiltration and found ethyl chloride spray to be significantly superior (*P*<0.05). Conversely, Lakshmanan and Ravindran^21^ found no significant difference between cryotherapy (ice pack) and 20% benzocaine gel for buccal infiltration pain in 30 paediatric patients.

**Cryotherapy as an adjunct to local anaesthetic injection.** Peedikayil and colleagues^22^ demonstrated that cryotherapy spray applied as an adjunct to inferior alveolar nerve block significantly reduced operative pain in teeth with molar-incisor hypomineralisation (n=28, split-mouth). A 2025 RCT evaluated intrapulpal injection of lidocaine cooled to 4--5 degrees C (cryoanesthesia) versus room temperature (25--26 degrees C) and found that the cooled solution achieved a 100% success rate versus 80% for room temperature, with significantly greater pain reduction (*P*<0.001).^23^

**Systematic reviews.** Tirupathi and Rajasekhar^24^ systematically reviewed six studies of pre-cooling before dental injection in children and found that four of five studies reporting VAS scores showed lower scores in the pre-cooling group. Amrollahi and colleagues^25^ conducted a similar systematic review reaching concordant conclusions. Honaje and colleagues^26^ performed a systematic review with meta-analysis of eight studies comparing ice with topical anaesthetic gel for intraoral injections in children and reported a pooled effect favouring equivalence.

### Domain 2: Vascular access procedures (n=12)

Twelve studies examined vapocoolant sprays versus local anaesthetic drugs for pain during venepuncture or intravenous cannulation.

**Systematic reviews and meta-analyses.** Wang and colleagues^27^ conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 RCTs involving 3143 participants and found that vapocoolant sprays significantly reduced pain in adults undergoing venous puncture (standardised mean difference [SMD] -0.65; 95% confidence interval [CI] -0.85 to -0.45; *P*<0.00001) but were ineffective in children. The Cochrane review by Griffith and colleagues^28^ found limited evidence for vapocoolant sprays during intravenous cannulation, with high heterogeneity among studies. Zhu and colleagues^29^ conducted a meta-analysis of vapocoolant spray versus placebo/no treatment for intravenous cannulation and found a significant pain reduction (weighted mean difference -0.97; 95% CI -1.40 to -0.53).

A 2025 network meta-analysis by BMC Anesthesiology^30^ compared nine local analgesia methods for venepuncture pain in children (40 RCTs, 4481 participants) and found that vapocoolant spray was no more effective than placebo, while EMLA cream, amethocaine, and needle-free lidocaine systems were significantly superior.

**Individual RCTs.** Mirfazaelian and colleagues^31^ directly compared vapocoolant spray with lidocaine-prilocaine cream for intravenous cannulation and found both modalities to be effective. Gur and Cakmak^32^ demonstrated that ethyl chloride spray significantly reduced venepuncture pain compared with placebo (VAS 4 vs 5; *P*<0.001) in 339 emergency department patients. Shah and colleagues^33^ compared subcutaneous lignocaine injection with vapocoolant spray for adult intravenous cannulation.

### Domain 3: Thoracic surgery and chest wall trauma (n=11)

This domain was characterised by the most substantial body of evidence for cryoneurolysis, but with a conspicuous absence of direct comparisons with local anaesthetic-based regional techniques.

**Systematic reviews.** Park and colleagues^34^ conducted a PRISMA-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 RCTs evaluating cryoanalgesia for postsurgical pain, predominantly in thoracotomy. Cryoanalgesia combined with opioids was superior to opioids alone for acute pain (mean difference [MD] -2.32; 95% CI -3.35 to -1.30) and persistent pain (MD -0.81; 95% CI -1.10 to -0.53). Cha and colleagues^35^ systematically reviewed 23 studies (570 patients) of intercostal cryoneurolysis for chest wall pain and identified five subgroups: pectus excavatum, thoracotomy, post-thoracotomy pain syndrome, malignant chest wall pain, and traumatic rib fractures. Most studies demonstrated decreased opioid use with cryoneurolysis.

**Recent RCTs.** A 2025 RCT published in the Journal of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery^36^ randomised patients undergoing minimally invasive thoracic surgery (VATS) to intercostal nerve cryoablation versus standard care and found lower pain scores at 12 hours in the cryoablation group (0 vs 2; *P*=0.04). However, pain scores were paradoxically higher at 5 weeks in the cryoablation group (4 vs 1; *P*=0.01), raising questions about delayed neuralgia. An intercostal cryoablation versus thoracic epidural analgesia trial protocol for Nuss repair of pectus excavatum (ICE trial) was published in BMJ Open in 2024,^37^ representing a rare direct comparison with a local anaesthetic-based modality.

**Rib fracture management.** Finneran and colleagues^38^ published a landmark RCT in Anesthesiology (2025) comparing ultrasound-guided percutaneous cryoneurolysis with an active control (single-injection ropivacaine intercostal nerve block) for traumatic rib fracture pain. This represents one of the only direct cryoneurolysis-versus-local-anaesthetic comparisons in the perioperative literature.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Miller and colleagues^39^ conducted a retrospective propensity-matched analysis of 23 138 patients from a US National All-Payer Database and found that intercostal nerve cryoablation during lobectomy reduced narcotic use and index hospitalisation costs.

### Domain 4: Orthopaedic surgery (n=6)

**Total knee arthroplasty (TKA).** Swisher and colleagues^40^ conducted a pilot RCT of ultrasound-guided percutaneous cryoneurolysis of the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve for post-TKA analgesia in 16 patients. The cryoneurolysis group reported slightly lower average and worst pain scores and opioid consumption at most time points between postoperative days 4 and 21, compared with the sham group. Radnovich and colleagues^41^ conducted a larger RCT of preoperative cryoneurolysis before TKA and found reduced postoperative pain and opioid use.

**Chronic knee pain.** A 2025 prospective RCT compared cryoneurolysis of genicular nerves with cooled radiofrequency ablation in 30 patients with knee osteoarthritis.^42^ Both groups showed significant VAS and WOMAC score reductions over 12 months, with a transient cryoneurolysis advantage at 6 months.

**Limitations.** No study in this domain directly compared cryoneurolysis with local anaesthetic-based peripheral nerve blocks (e.g. adductor canal block, femoral nerve block) for perioperative TKA analgesia, which represents a significant evidence gap.

### Domain 5: Dermatological procedures (n=4)

Roongpisuthipong and colleagues^43^ conducted a rater-blinded RCT comparing ice packs with topical lidocaine-prilocaine mixture for pain control during axillary laser hair removal (88 treatments). Participants reported lower immediate post-treatment VAS scores with ice (*P*=0.03), although ice scores were higher at 5 minutes (*P*=0.03). Overall, 60.2% of participants preferred ice. Kuwahara and Skinner^44^ compared EMLA cream with ice for injection-related pain in a dermatological setting and found both to be effective. Gupta and colleagues^45^ compared EMLA cream, cryogel, and lignocaine spray for various dermatological procedures (molluscum extraction, intralesional injection, radiofrequency ablation, dermabrasion) and reported that all modalities were superior to no treatment, with variable relative efficacy depending on the procedure.

### Supply chain context

No study among the 47 included explicitly examined cryoanalgesia as a contingency strategy during local anaesthetic drug shortages. However, several studies implicitly highlighted the relevance: Finneran and Ilfeld^14^ noted the advantage of cryoneurolysis in providing prolonged analgesia without dependence on drug supply chains; Biel and colleagues^12^ described cryoneurolysis as providing "a comparable quality of regional analgesia to local anaesthetic-based interventions" with a substantially longer duration of effect; and the IPSIS shortage advisory^10^ called for evaluation of non-pharmacological alternatives.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This scoping review mapped the existing evidence comparing cryoanalgesia with local anaesthetic drugs across five clinical domains. The key findings are: (i) for superficial pre-procedural analgesia (dental pre-injection, vascular access), surface cooling modalities (ice, vapocoolant spray) are broadly non-inferior to topical local anaesthetic drugs, with the strongest evidence in dental applications; (ii) for perioperative analgesia, intercostal cryoneurolysis reduces opioid consumption after thoracic surgery, but direct comparisons with local anaesthetic nerve blocks are almost entirely absent; (iii) percutaneous cryoneurolysis shows promise in orthopaedic and trauma settings, with one landmark RCT directly comparing cryoneurolysis with ropivacaine intercostal nerve block; and (iv) no study has explicitly evaluated cryoanalgesia as a supply chain contingency, representing a major research opportunity.

### Evidence landscape and gaps

The evidence is heavily concentrated in two domains --- dental procedures and vascular access --- where surface cooling is compared with topical local anaesthetic formulations. These comparisons, while informative, address only a narrow subset of clinical scenarios affected by local anaesthetic shortages. The most clinically impactful shortages involve injectable formulations used for infiltration anaesthesia, peripheral nerve blocks, and neuraxial techniques.^2,3^

For deeper applications (nerve blocks, neuraxial), the conceptual comparator shifts from surface cooling to cryoneurolysis. Here, the evidence base is growing rapidly --- particularly in thoracic surgery and TKA --- but is almost exclusively structured as cryoneurolysis versus opioids or standard care, not versus local anaesthetic-based techniques. The Finneran 2025 rib fracture RCT^38^ and the ICE trial protocol^37^ are notable exceptions that directly compare cryoneurolysis with regional anaesthesia modalities.

Several important gaps warrant attention. First, no studies were identified comparing cryoanalgesia with local anaesthetics for minor surgical procedures (wound closure, skin biopsy, abscess drainage) that constitute a large proportion of local anaesthetic use. Second, cost-effectiveness data are limited to a single retrospective study in thoracic surgery.^39^ Third, patient satisfaction and acceptability data are sparse but consistently favour cryoanalgesia where reported.^43^ Fourth, safety data are generally reassuring, with cryoneurolysis characterised by reversible sensory loss and minimal systemic effects,^12^ but the 5-week pain rebound observed in one thoracic surgery RCT requires further investigation.^36^

### Supply chain resilience and policy implications

The pharmaceutical supply chain for local anaesthetics exhibits several structural vulnerabilities. The concentration of generic manufacturing in India exposes the EU to episodic supply shocks triggered by GCP and GMP violations.^5-8^ The EMA's suspension of nearly 400 generic medicines in 2023 following the Synapse Labs GCP violations^6,7^ and 700 medicines in 2015 following GVK Biosciences data integrity failures^5^ demonstrate that regulatory actions at single facilities can have cascading supply effects. In the United States, the just-in-time inventory system amplifies the impact of any manufacturing disruption.^2^

Cryoanalgesic modalities offer several supply chain advantages. Ice is universally available and essentially free. Vapocoolant sprays have simpler manufacturing requirements and supply chains than injectable pharmaceutical formulations. Cryoneurolysis devices are reusable capital equipment not subject to pharmaceutical supply chain disruptions. These characteristics make cryoanalgesia a rational component of institutional contingency planning for drug shortages.

We propose a tiered framework for cryoanalgesia implementation during local anaesthetic shortages (Table 3):

- **Tier 1 (Immediate implementation, strong evidence):** Ice or vapocoolant spray for dental pre-injection analgesia; vapocoolant spray for adult venepuncture/cannulation.
- **Tier 2 (Conditional implementation, moderate evidence):** Ice packs for dermatological procedures; intercostal cryoneurolysis during thoracic surgery (where equipment is available).
- **Tier 3 (Research priority, insufficient evidence):** Cryoneurolysis as an alternative to peripheral nerve blocks for surgical anaesthesia; cryoanalgesia for minor outpatient procedures.

### Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths: comprehensive search across five databases; inclusion of all study designs to maximise the breadth of evidence capture; organisation by clinical domain to facilitate clinical applicability; and explicit contextualisation within the supply chain resilience framework. Limitations include the inherent constraints of scoping review methodology (no formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analytic pooling); potential publication bias; restriction to English-language publications; and the possibility that some relevant studies were indexed under terms not captured by our search strategy. Additionally, the heterogeneity of cryoanalgesic modalities (from ice application to sophisticated cryoneurolysis devices) makes direct comparisons across domains challenging.

### Future research directions

Based on the gaps identified, we recommend the following research priorities:

1. **Pragmatic RCTs** comparing cryoneurolysis with local anaesthetic nerve blocks in perioperative settings, particularly for thoracic surgery (intercostal), knee arthroplasty (adductor canal/genicular), and rib fracture management.
2. **Feasibility studies** evaluating cryoanalgesia for minor surgical procedures (wound closure, skin biopsy, abscess drainage) where local anaesthetic infiltration is standard.
3. **Cost-effectiveness analyses** incorporating supply chain disruption scenarios, comparing direct costs, indirect costs of procedure delays during shortages, and quality-adjusted outcomes.
4. **Institutional preparedness studies** evaluating the implementation of cryoanalgesia contingency protocols during simulated or actual drug shortage events.
5. **Patient preference studies** comparing acceptability of cryoanalgesic versus pharmacological approaches across diverse populations and clinical settings.

### Target journal considerations

The clinical and policy significance of this review positions it for submission to multiple journal categories. Table 4 presents a comparative analysis of potential target journals.

**Specialist anaesthesia journals.** The British Journal of Anaesthesia (BJA; impact factor 9.2) publishes systematic reviews of up to 5000 words with 150 references, and its scope encompasses pain medicine and perioperative care. The review's focus on local anaesthetic alternatives aligns directly with the BJA readership. Anaesthesia (impact factor 9.1) and Anesthesiology (impact factor 8.8) represent high-impact alternatives with similar formatting requirements.

**General medical journals.** The BMJ (impact factor 93.6) has published editorials on drug shortages^11^ and the PRISMA 2020 statement,^46^ signalling interest in supply chain resilience and review methodology. BMJ Open (impact factor 2.3) accepts systematic and scoping reviews with no word limit and has published multiple scoping review protocols and completed reviews in anaesthesia-related topics.^47^ The Lancet (impact factor 98.4) publishes reviews on health policy and systems topics; the supply chain framing could appeal if positioned as a health systems resilience paper.

**Pain and regional anaesthesia journals.** Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine (RAPM; impact factor 9.3) and Pain (impact factor 5.9) would be appropriate if the manuscript emphasises cryoneurolysis as a regional anaesthesia technique.

**Drug safety and policy journals.** Pharmacoepidemiology and Drug Safety recently published a scoping review on the impact of supply chain disruptions on drug utilisation,^48^ suggesting receptivity to supply-chain-themed reviews.

**Recommended submission strategy:** We recommend primary submission to the BJA, given the direct relevance to anaesthesia practice, the established scope for systematic reviews, and the appropriate word and reference limits. If desk-rejected, the supply chain resilience framing could be strengthened for resubmission to BMJ Open (open access, broad readership) or as a Policy Review to Anaesthesia. The health systems angle also supports submission to BMJ as an Analysis paper.

## Conclusions

This scoping review demonstrates that cryoanalgesia encompasses a spectrum of modalities with varying levels of evidence supporting their use as alternatives to local anaesthetic drugs. Surface cooling techniques (ice, vapocoolant spray) have robust evidence for dental pre-injection analgesia and adult venepuncture. Cryoneurolysis is an emerging modality for perioperative analgesia with growing RCT evidence, but critical direct comparisons with local anaesthetic nerve blocks remain scarce. The intersection of cryoanalgesia and pharmaceutical supply chain resilience is an entirely unmapped research territory. Given the structural vulnerabilities of local anaesthetic supply chains and the increasing frequency of shortage events, we recommend that cryoanalgesia be investigated as a contingency strategy and integrated into institutional preparedness planning.
